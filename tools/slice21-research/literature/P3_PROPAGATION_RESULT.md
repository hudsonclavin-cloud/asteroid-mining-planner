--- RAW PERPLEXITY ANSWER BELOW ---

Comparable mission-design tools converge on the same pattern: an adaptive high-order embedded Runge–Kutta numerical propagator wrapped around a modular force-model stack, with lower-fidelity analytical or Kepler/Lambert propagators used for screening and initial guesses rather than final dynamics. For a browser mission-planning tool that starts heliocentric and later expands to cislunar, the best architectural choice is a force-model-agnostic Cowell propagator with adaptive Dormand–Prince as the default, plus a clean upgrade path to CR3BP and full ephemeris n-body propagation rather than baking patched-conic assumptions into the core state model.[1][2][3]

## Architecture GMAT separates the propagator from the force model, lets the user choose among several adaptive Runge–Kutta integrators, and explicitly recommends PrinceDormand78 as its “best all purpose integrator” while noting that different tolerances and error-control settings change the cost/accuracy trade materially. Orekit does the same at the API level: `NumericalPropagator` is an integrated propagator configured with an initial state plus pluggable force models, event detectors, Jacobians, and additional derivatives, which is exactly the separation you want if Aster will grow from heliocentric NEA transfers into cislunar work. Poliastro is lighter-weight but structurally similar: its Cowell propagator numerically integrates the Cartesian state and lets you inject custom perturbation accelerations, using DOP853 through SciPy as the general numerical workhorse.[2][3][1] For a browser implementation, keep three layers separate: state representation, integrator, and force providers. Concretely, use Cartesian state in inertial frames for the core integrator, keep every perturbation as an acceleration callback \(a(t,\mathbf r,\mathbf v,\text{params})\), and make frame/ephemeris access a dependency of the force layer rather than of the integrator itself. That avoids a heliocentric dead end because the same integrator can later propagate Sun-centered, Earth-centered, barycentric, or CR3BP states so long as the force callback and frame transforms are swapped.[3][2]

## Integrators RK4 fixed-step is attractive only for simplicity and determinism; the comparable tools above do not use it as their primary high-fidelity engine, and their documented defaults instead center on adaptive embedded Runge–Kutta families such as DP45, DP78, DOP853, and RK89. GMAT exposes PrinceDormand45, PrinceDormand78, PrinceDormand853, and RungeKutta89, with default numerical settings including initial step 60 seconds, minimum step 0.001 seconds, maximum step 2700 seconds, maximum step attempts 50, and accuracy \(10^{-11}\) for most integrators. Poliastro’s Cowell path uses DOP853 with default relative tolerance \(10^{-11}\), and when multiple output epochs are requested it propagates once to the maximum time and uses dense output to recover the intermediate states, which is a very relevant performance pattern for browser trajectory previews.[1][2] For JS/f64, the practical choice is DP5(4) for interactive coarse work and DOP853-style 8(5,3) for validation-grade or longer-arc propagation. DP5(4) usually wins on implementation simplicity and lower per-step stage count, while DOP853 tends to win when you need larger stable steps at the same tolerance over smooth long arcs, which is why it appears both in poliastro and among GMAT’s recommended adaptive families. I would not make RK4 the production default except perhaps for pedagogy, reproducible demos, or tightly bounded low-order preview mode, because fixed-step tuning becomes the user’s burden and the step that is safe near periapsis or close-approach is often wasteful elsewhere.[2][1] The main adaptive-step pitfalls are exactly the ones GMAT warns about: too-tight tolerance under a stringent error metric can crush performance without meaningful state improvement, and a nonzero enforced minimum step can cause the integrator to fail to satisfy the requested accuracy. In browser code, also avoid naive per-component absolute error scaling because heliocentric position components can be large while velocity or small third-body-induced differences are the real quantities of interest; embedded RK only works well if the norm and scaling reflect the mission regime. A robust pattern is a mixed relative/absolute controller with separate floors for position and velocity, hard caps on growth/shrink factors, rejection count limits, and dense-output interpolation so the UI can sample at user epochs without forcing the solver to land exactly on every displayed timestamp.[1][2]

## Perturbation ROI For heliocentric NEA transfers, the force-model ladder should start with Sun point-mass only, then add planetary third bodies for the bodies that actually dominate the arc geometry, then SRP when area-to-mass makes it non-negligible, and only later niche effects like relativistic terms or small-body harmonics near operations. The GMAT acceptance plan explicitly validates point-mass perturbations, harmonic gravity, SRP, drag, tides, and combined-force cases across Earth, Moon, planets, deep-space, and libration-point regimes, which is a strong signal that mature tools treat these as composable rather than monolithic models.[4][2] For cislunar trajectories, the ROI ordering changes fast: Earth point-mass alone is inadequate once lunar dynamics shape the transfer, and patched-conic logic loses authority because Sun and Moon third-body forcing is no longer a small correction to a dominant single-center field. Orekit users discussing meter-class LEO validation recommend high-order gravity, drag, SRP, Sun/Moon third-body terms, and even tides for that regime, while also stating that Orekit numerical models have been validated to well below meter accuracy over spans longer than one revolution; that illustrates both the maturity of the architecture and the fact that force priorities are regime-specific.[5][3][4] Magnitude-wise, I need to flag that the tools retrieved here do not publish a single canonical table of heliocentric-NEA and cislunar acceleration magnitudes, so the estimates below are third-party-estimated, not official-published. Still, the design ordering is stable: for heliocentric cruise, planetary third-body terms dominate when you are near an encounter geometry, while SRP often matters before relativistic corrections if area-to-mass is not tiny; for cislunar transfers, Earth \(J_2\) matters near Earth, lunar and solar third-body terms matter throughout translunar space, and SRP is usually secondary unless you are flying a high area-to-mass vehicle. [third-party-estimated] A useful engineering ladder is:
- Heliocentric NEA transfers: Sun point mass first; add Earth/Mars/Venus/Jupiter third bodies when their geometry is relevant; add SRP for high area-to-mass or long low-thrust/coast sensitivity studies; add asteroid gravity/harmonics only for proximity ops. [third-party-estimated]
- Cislunar: Earth point mass is never enough for serious design; add Moon point mass immediately, then Sun third body, then Earth \(J_2\) for near-Earth departure/return arcs, then lunar gravity refinement and SRP if the mission stays long enough or sensitivity warrants it. [third-party-estimated] A rough scale, flagged as third-party-estimated, is that at 1 AU SRP on a cannonball spacecraft is on the order of \(10^{-7}\) to \(10^{-8}\,\text{m/s}^2\) depending on area-to-mass and reflectivity; Earth \(J_2\) is dominant in LEO but falls rapidly with distance; and Sun/Moon third-body terms become mission-shaping in cislunar space because the transfer spends substantial time where no single two-body attractor cleanly dominates. [third-party-estimated] That is enough to justify an ROI rule for Aster: heliocentric screening can stay patched-conic-plus-selected-perturbations longer, but cislunar screening should switch much earlier to either CR3BP-informed initial guesses or full ephemeris n-body propagation. [third-party-estimated][3][4]

## Breakdown regimes Patched-conic begins to break down when the trajectory’s qualitative structure depends on multi-body invariant manifolds, weak stability boundaries, ballistic capture, long residence in regions where the Earth and Moon accelerations are of comparable order, or when small timing changes move you across separatrices rather than just perturbing a conic arc. GMAT’s own test program includes libration-point cases, and Orekit exposes a full numerical propagator architecture rather than assuming sphere-of-influence stitching, which reflects what serious tools do once cislunar or libration dynamics are in play.[4][3] For browser planning, use three fidelity modes rather than one “truth” engine. Mode 1: patched-conic/Lambert for global search and pruning; Mode 2: full ephemeris Cowell n-body for candidate refinement; Mode 3: CR3BP or bicircular-style dynamical models for cislunar design spaces where manifold structure matters and patched-conic intuition is actively misleading. Full n-body becomes necessary when you need Earth-Moon-Sun timing fidelity for translunar injection, lunar flybys, NRHO-like environments, or captures/escapes sensitive to phase, while CR3BP becomes necessary when the design variable itself is a family of multi-body trajectories rather than a patched sequence of conic legs. [third-party-estimated][2][3][4]

## Validation fixtures GMAT is the richest oracle source among the tools retrieved: its acceptance-test documentation describes force-model tests run independently and in combination, across Earth, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Neptune, Pluto, Saturn, Uranus, Venus, libration-point, and deep-space cases, plus separate integrator and stopping-condition tests. The same GMAT documentation states that the force models tested included harmonic gravity, point-mass perturbations, atmospheric drag variants, SRP, tides, and relativity, and later GMAT V&V documentation describes “closure” tests where a state is integrated forward and then backward to compare against the initial condition. Those make excellent oracle fixtures because they let you separate force-model correctness from integrator correctness.[6][4] Orekit’s public API docs are strong on architecture but lighter on an official compact validation catalog in the retrieved pages; still, the forum discussion states that Orekit numerical propagation has been validated to well below meter accuracy over spans longer than one revolution for relevant LEO use cases, and Sentinel-6A SP3 data is suggested there as a real-world external validation source. Poliastro’s docs are best used as transparent algorithmic oracles rather than institutional truth data: its Cowell+DOP853 path and its published examples for custom perturbations, including \(J_2\), are useful for regression comparisons against your own TS implementation on identical force models and tolerances.[5][2] The fixture set I would build is:
- Integrator closure tests, forward then backward on pure two-body and on smooth perturbed cases, following GMAT’s published validation philosophy.[6]
- Cross-tool state matching on a months-long heliocentric Sun-only arc, then Sun+selected planets, against poliastro Cowell DOP853.[2]
- Earth departure and translunar arc comparisons against GMAT and Orekit with the same ephemerides and force toggles.[3][1]
- Regime-separation tests: a case where patched-conic agrees, one where full n-body moves the arrival geometry materially, and one cislunar case where CR3BP structure explains the divergence.[4]

## Runtime envelope The retrieved sources do not publish browser-specific runtime benchmarks, so any JS/WASM performance figures here are third-party-estimated rather than official-published. In practice, a single spacecraft propagated for months with an adaptive DP5(4) or DOP853 class method and a modest force stack is entirely plausible client-side in f64, especially if force evaluation is the bottleneck rather than the RK tableau algebra. [third-party-estimated] The big runtime drivers are not JavaScript itself so much as ephemeris lookup cost, event detection, frame transforms, and whether the UI demands many output samples or just sparse solver checkpoints with dense interpolation. [third-party-estimated][3][2] A reasonable planning budget, flagged third-party-estimated, is:
- Sun-only or Sun+few-point-masses months-long single-arc propagation: interactive on modern hardware. [third-party-estimated]
- Earth-Moon-Sun plus \(J_2\) departure/return arcs: still browser-feasible for one spacecraft, but you should expect force evaluation and rejection bursts near close approaches to dominate cost. [third-party-estimated]
- Large Monte Carlo or porkchop-wide propagation grids: move to Web Workers, WASM kernels, or server/offline precompute. [third-party-estimated] LEVEL 1 — the three most decision-relevant follow-ups are below.

## Level 1

### Follow-up 1 Why this matters for the tool: it determines whether Aster should ship one default integrator or a dual-mode propagation stack. Use two adaptive integrators, not one: DP5(4) as the fast interactive default and DOP853-class as the high-accuracy/refinement mode. GMAT exposes both 5(4) and higher-order Prince–Dormand families and explicitly recommends PD78 as a strong all-purpose option, while poliastro standardizes its Cowell path on DOP853, which is a strong signal that serious tools keep a high-order adaptive mode available even when cheaper options exist. The numbers directly published here are official-published: GMAT default `Accuracy` \(10^{-11}\), `InitialStepSize` 60 s, `MinStep` 0.001 s, `MaxStep` 2700 s, `MaxStepAttempts` 50; poliastro default Cowell `rtol` \(10^{-11}\). [official-published][1][2]

### Follow-up 2 Why this matters for the tool: it decides whether cislunar support can be bolted onto the heliocentric engine later or must shape the core architecture now. Cislunar support should shape the core now, specifically by keeping the integrator frame-agnostic and the force model ephemeris-driven rather than central-body-driven. Orekit’s `NumericalPropagator` design already assumes this modularity, and GMAT’s validation scope spans deep space and libration-point cases, which means comparable tools do not hardwire a single-center worldview into their propagator core. The official-published numbers here are structural rather than performance numbers: Orekit’s default propagation parameterization is equinoctial with true longitude, and GMAT’s acceptance plan includes dedicated libration-point test sections. [official-published][4][3]

### Follow-up 3 Why this matters for the tool: it decides what “validated” means for an in-browser propagator and how much trust users can place in displayed trajectories. Validation should be layered: algorithmic equivalence against poliastro on matched force models, acceptance-style closure tests inspired by GMAT, and selective real-world or external-ephemeris comparisons for Earth-orbit and cislunar cases. GMAT’s published V&V approach includes forward/backward closure tests, and Orekit users point to external SP3 products such as Sentinel-6A as practical truth data for some regimes. The official-published numbers are that Orekit users describe validation “well below meter accuracy” over spans longer than one revolution for numerical propagation, while the cited LEO requirement example in the forum was 75 m after 1 revolution with 10 m position and 3 mm/s velocity initial uncertainty. [official-published][5][6][2][4]

## Level 2

### Follow-up 1.1 Why this matters for the tool: the dual-integrator recommendation only pays off if the mode-switch criterion is objective rather than guesswork. The clean switch criterion is not mission label but estimated solver work per accepted output span: stay in DP5(4) while acceptance ratios are high and step sizes remain comfortably above your display-sampling cadence, then promote to DOP853-class when tighter tolerances or rapidly varying force geometry drive repeated rejections or very small accepted steps. [third-party-estimated] GMAT’s warnings about over-tight accuracy settings and nonzero minimum steps tell you where this switch becomes necessary: once tighter settings increase runtime without meaningful solution change, you are paying for the wrong mode or the wrong error metric. The official-published numbers supporting the policy are again GMAT’s `MinStep` 0.001 s, `MaxStepAttempts` 50, and its recommendation to start around \(10^{-9}\) and tighten by decades experimentally to find the performance/accuracy knee for the application. [official-published][1][2]

### Follow-up 2.1 Why this matters for the tool: “frame-agnostic” is only useful if it leads to a concrete internal API that survives the heliocentric-to-cislunar transition. The decisive API choice is to propagate a Cartesian state plus epoch in a declared inertial frame, with every force model responsible for any body ephemeris queries and frame-relative quantities it needs, while the propagator only sees \(\dot{\mathbf y}=f(t,\mathbf y)\). That matches Orekit’s design, where force models and event detectors are attached to the numerical propagator rather than entangled with a specific central-body propagation law. No additional official-published numeric constants are introduced by this decision beyond Orekit’s documented default orbital parameterization; the rest is architectural, not numeric.[3]

### Follow-up 3.1 Why this matters for the tool: validation scope directly determines engineering cost and the credibility of any “high-fidelity” label. The single most valuable oracle fixture family is GMAT-style closure testing because it isolates numerical integration defects without needing external truth data for every regime. Add matched-force cross-checks against poliastro for heliocentric and simple perturbed cases because poliastro’s Cowell+DOP853 path is transparent and easy to reproduce exactly. The official-published numbers here are GMAT’s example closure-test duration of 1 day for a LEO integrator case with Earth 20x20 gravity, Sun, Moon, MSISE90 density, and SRP, as described in later V&V documentation. [official-published][6][2]

## Level 3

### Follow-up 2.1.1 Why this matters for the tool: once the API is frame-agnostic, the next load-bearing decision is whether to center the numerical state on a dominant body anyway for performance. Do not make “central body” a required part of the numerical core state; make it metadata for convenience views, not a governing assumption of the equations of motion. That preserves one propagation engine across heliocentric screening, Earth-centered departure, translunar coast, and cislunar multi-body refinement, while letting patched-conic and CR3BP live as separate modeling modes above the same state/integrator substrate. The official-published support is qualitative but strong: Orekit’s numerical propagator is configured by force models and state setup, and GMAT’s validation corpus spans deep-space and libration-point cases rather than only classical central-body orbits.[4][3]
